## 1. The Worry Hill

- **Kind:** Tool
- **For:** Child and parent
- **Summary:** Why worry goes up, why it comes down if you stay, and what stops it coming down.

**Video:** The Worry Hill (from the key concept videos, when it is ready).

**Text — for the child**

When you face something scary, your Fear Level climbs. It feels like it will keep climbing forever.
It doesn't. If you stay, it reaches the top of the hill and then comes down by itself.

Getting away from the scary thing, or doing something to feel safe, stops the climb early. You feel
better for a moment, but you never get to the other side of the hill. So your brain never learns that
the Fear Level comes down on its own. Next time, the hill is just as big.

Each time you stay and get over the hill, the next hill is a bit smaller. That is what exposures are
for.

**Image:** the Worry Hill: Fear Level rising, peaking and coming down over time, with a "stop sign"
at the top where a safety behavior cuts it off.

**Text — for the parent**

Your child's anxiety rises when they face a trigger, peaks, and then falls if they stay in the
situation. Avoiding, escaping, or a safety behavior (including ones you do for them) brings quick
relief. That relief is what keeps the anxiety going: your child's brain learns that the situation was
dangerous and that the escape is what made it OK.

Exposures work the other way. Staying in the situation until the Fear Level drops teaches the brain
that the situation is safe enough, and that the anxiety passes on its own.

## 2. The Candy Jar

- **Kind:** Tool
- **For:** Child and parent
- **Summary:** How exposures change what your brain expects, one "safe" memory at a time.

**Video:** The Candy Jar (from the key concept videos, when it is ready).

**Text — for the child**

Imagine your brain has a candy jar for each thing that scares you. Every time something felt scary or
dangerous, a red candy went in. For things that worry you, the jar has lots of red candies. So when
you meet that situation, a red candy is what pops out first, and you feel scared.

Every time you face the situation and nothing terrible happens, a green candy goes in. One green
candy doesn't change much. But with enough of them, the green ones start to outnumber the red. Then
a green one pops out first, and the situation stops feeling so scary.

Each exposure adds green candies.

**Image:** a jar with mostly red candies, then the same jar after several exposures, with more green
than red.

**Text — for the parent**

An anxious mind has stored more "danger" memories about a situation than "safe" ones, so the danger
memory is the one that comes to mind first. Exposures don't erase the danger memories. They add new
"safe" ones. With enough safe memories for a situation, those are the ones that come to mind, and the
anxiety is less.

This is why exposures are repeated, and why the same situation is practiced more than once.

## 3. The Fear Level

- **Kind:** Tool
- **For:** Child and parent
- **Summary:** A number from 1 to 10 for how worried you feel, so everyone means the same thing.

**Question:** your list calls this "Distress thermometer". The app calls the number "Fear Level"
everywhere people read it (decided 2026-09-10). Should the page be called "The Fear Level", or "The
Fear Level thermometer"?

**Video:** Distress Thermometer (from the key concept videos, when it is ready).

**Text — for the child**

Your Fear Level is a number for how worried or scared you feel right now.

- **1** means not worried at all.
- **10** means the most worried you have ever been.

There are no wrong answers. Your number is your number. You'll use it before and after exposures, and
when someone asks "What's your Fear Level?". Over time you'll see the numbers come down.

**Image:** a thermometer from 1 to 10, with words beside a few numbers.

**Question:** the clinician guide says 1 to 10. The education module diagram is planned as 0 to 10
(`docs/plans/education-redesign.md`). Which is it?

**Text — for the parent**

The Fear Level gives you and your child a shared way to talk about anxiety without arguing about how
bad something is. Ask for the number and accept it. You don't need to agree with it or talk them out
of it.

A good way to ask: "Is [nickname] here?", then "What's your Fear Level?".

## 4. Your nickname for the worry

- **Kind:** Tool
- **For:** Child and parent
- **Summary:** Give your worry a name, so you can talk about it as something separate from you.

**Video:** Nickname the Fear (from the key concept videos, when it is ready).

**Text — for the child**

Your worry is not you. It's something that shows up and tries to boss you around. Giving it a name
makes it easier to spot, and easier to talk about.

Pick a name that is friendly or a bit silly. Names other kids have picked: Worry Bug, Mr. What-if,
Panic Penguin, Belly Butterflies.

When it shows up, you can say hi to it: "Hi, Worry Bug. I know you're here." You don't have to fight
it or make it leave. Just notice it.

You don't have to choose the name today. You can think about it.

**Text — for the parent**

Use your child's nickname calmly and with acceptance, not as a joke at their expense. When you see
anxiety coming up, you can ask "Is [nickname] here?", then "What's your Fear Level?".

Naming it helps your child notice the anxiety and talk about it, instead of being swept up by it.

## 5. Rewards

- **Kind:** Tool
- **For:** Child and parent
- **Summary:** A reward soon after hard work makes it easier to do, and helps the learning stick.

**Question:** your list is one line for this. Should the page cover points as well as rewards? Points
for every age is in the Dr. Walker review queue, so this draft leaves points out.

**Video:** Rewards (from the key concept videos, when it is ready).

**Text — for the child**

Exposures are hard work. When you do one, or practice a tool, you earn a reward.

You help choose the rewards, so they're things you actually want. The sooner you get the reward after
the hard thing, the better it works.

**Text — for the parent**

Rewards do two things. They motivate your child to do something hard, and, when they come promptly,
they help the learning from the exposure stick.

- **Make the list with your child.** Choose rewards that matter to them, not what you think should
  be enough.
- **Give it as soon as you can after the exposure or practice.** A reward days later does much less.
- **Reward the effort, not the Fear Level.** Your child earns it by doing the task, even if it was
  hard and the number stayed high.

## 6. Mottos

- **Kind:** Strategy
- **For:** Child and parent
- **Summary:** Short sayings to use when the worry tells you something bad is going to happen.

**Text — for the child**

Worry is good at guessing. It guesses that bad things will happen, and it sounds very sure. A motto is
a short saying you can tell yourself when that happens. It helps you notice the worry is guessing, and
that you don't have to believe the guess.

Pick the one that works best for you, and practice it with your clinician first.

**Text — for the parent**

Mottos help your child answer the worry's predictions without needing reassurance from you. When your
child asks you whether something bad will happen, you can remind them of their motto instead of
answering the question.

**Examples**

1. **"Wait and see"**
   How it's used: When the worry says something bad is going to happen, say "Wait and see." You don't
   know yet. Do the thing, and find out what really happens.

2. **"Can't know"**
   How it's used: When the worry wants you to be sure everything will be OK, say "Can't know." Nobody
   can know for certain what will happen. You can do it anyway, without being sure.

3. **"What else could happen?"**
   How it's used: When the worry tells you the worst thing will happen, ask "What else could happen?"
   Think of two or three other ways it could go. Which is most likely?

## 7. Take 5

- **Kind:** Strategy
- **For:** Child and parent
- **Summary:** When worry is high, wait five minutes before you do or say anything.

**Text — for the child**

When your worry is triggered, the part of your brain that sounds the alarm (the amygdala) takes over.
It's hard to think clearly or make good choices while it's going off.

So take 5:

1. Set a timer for 5 minutes.
2. Don't do or say anything about the worry until it goes off.
3. When it does, check: do you still feel the same? What's your Fear Level now?
4. If you need more time, take another 5.

**Text — for the parent**

Take 5 also works for you and your child together. If a conversation about anxiety is going nowhere,
or getting heated, either of you can call "Take 5". Set a timer, stop talking about it, and come back
when it goes off.

The point is that nobody acts while the alarm is going off. After 5 minutes, check whether the concern
still feels the same.

## 8. Polite ignoring

- **Kind:** Strategy
- **For:** Parent only (never shown to the child)
- **Summary:** What to do when your child keeps asking you to accommodate their anxiety.

**Text — for the parent**

Sometimes your child will keep asking for an accommodation after you've decided to stop it: asking
for reassurance again and again, or asking you to do something for them. Polite ignoring is a way to
not give in, and not argue either.

Your clinician will go through this with you and your child before you use it, so everyone knows what
to expect.
